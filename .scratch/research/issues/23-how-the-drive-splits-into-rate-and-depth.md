## Answer

One primary study on hand gives the split under a rising chemical drive in man: Clark and von Euler 1972, seven healthy men rebreathing from a bell of oxygen, a hypercapnic ramp under hyperoxia. Up to a tidal volume of 1.5 to 2 times its resting value, inspiration's length stays fixed, so depth carries the whole rise and the rate stays at rest (range 1). Above that volume inspiration shortens as a hyperbola of tidal volume and expiration follows it, so depth and rate rise together (range 2), over the volumes their fit covered. They also state that Hey 1966 described ventilation as linear in tidal volume (p. 291). The Answer writes each as a form a law can state, inferred from the source with two model choices the source does not make (The split as a law can state it), and picks Clark and von Euler's ranges for body 38, a model default the coordinator confirms or overrules. It replaces the earlier model default, which put a ceiling on depth at the textbooks' inflation-reflex volume and let rate carry everything above it. The table below gives each work's tier, claim by claim where one work holds claims of different strength. An inference has no tier and is labeled. Two boxes are ticked: the second, for the rebreathing ranges Clark and von Euler measured, and the fifth, as a form inferred from their Table 1 with the two model choices. The first lacks hypoxic steps and hypercapnic steps from rest, the third the arms and the drive in sleep, the fourth the rows behind the rest's tidal volume and rate, and the sixth a measured bound on depth under a chemical drive.

### Rate and tidal volume over hypercapnic and hypoxic steps from rest

Hypercapnia, as a ramp and not a step: Clark and von Euler 1972 (primary, 7 healthy men, hyperoxic rebreathing to an end-tidal CO2 of 8%, spirometer, pp. 269 to 270). Tidal volume rose with the rate held until it reached 1.5 to 2 times its resting value (range 1, p. 267), then rate rose with it along Table 1's range 2 (p. 288). Ventilation in range 1 is the resting rate times tidal volume; in range 2 it is `V_T (V_T - V_0) / (C_h (1 + n) + (V_T - V_0) p)` (p. 288). In one man's plot of ventilation against inspiratory volume the highest point lies near 1.75 L/s at about 2.5 L (Fig. 10a, p. 288, read from the plot). The study reports the range constants and one man's plots, not a table of every man's rate and tidal volume. On range 1 it differs from Hey 1966 as Clark and von Euler describe it: in the examples Hey and colleagues showed, rate appeared to rise as soon as ventilation rose, which Clark and von Euler put down to those runs starting after ventilation had already risen (p. 293). That is their statement of another work's examples, not a reading of Hey, which cannot be had. The runs measured the whole response under hyperoxia and do not isolate an arm (The arms and sleep).

Hypoxia: missing. No work on hand gives rate and tidal volume under a hypoxic step. Van den Bosch 2021 (a review, p. 8) gives only the variability of ventilation and tidal volume under isocapnic hypoxia. Dahan 1990 recorded both breath by breath under end-tidal CO2 steps in nine resting men and published ventilation only. The box asks for both steps from rest: a primary study of rate and tidal volume under an isocapnic hypoxic step would close its hypoxic half, and one under a hypercapnic step from rest its other half, which Clark and von Euler's ramp does not.

### Whether inspiration's length or expiration's carries the rate change

Both, with expiration following inspiration, over the rebreathing ranges Clark and von Euler measured. Clark and von Euler 1972 (primary, man, hyperoxic rebreathing): above range 1, inspiration's length shortens as tidal volume rises (p. 280), and expiration's length is linear in inspiration's, `T_E = n T_I + p`, with `n` usually 0.5 to 1.0 and `p` close to zero in man (p. 283; one man's `n` 1.02, Fig. 9a, p. 286, its caption p. 287). In range 1, both lengths stay at their rest values as a cluster on the same line (p. 283). This is the parallel fall that Douglas 1982 cites them for (PDF p. 4), and it matches the sleep studies' unchanged share at rest (Douglas PDF p. 4; Stradling PDF pp. 3 to 4).

Inspiration's share of the breath, from `T_E = n T_I + p`, is `T_I / T_TOT = 1 / (1 + n + p / T_I)`. It is fixed only where `p` is zero, at `1 / (1 + n)`. With `p` kept it moves as `T_I` does: at `n` 1 and `p` 0.1 s it is 0.484 at a `T_I` of 1.5 s and 0.469 at 0.75 s. Setting `p` to zero is a model choice, since Clark and von Euler give `p` only as close to zero in man (p. 283), and it gives 0.5 to 0.67 by their `n`. The share at rest in the works on hand is lower: 0.367 awake in Stradling's six men (p. 366) and a median of 0.41 in Motamedi-Fakhr's 31 seated adults (p. 6). Clark and von Euler did not report the share. These are different people, postures and stimuli, so the gap does not show the share rising with the drive in one person. Harbour 2022 (a review, exercise, p. 4) gives a rise from about 40% at rest to 50% or more at maximal exercise, from the works it cites. The default holds the share at the rest's, which keeps the engine's one phase speed. A primary study that reports `T_I / T_TOT` under a CO2 step from rest would settle it.

### The arms and sleep

The central and the peripheral arm: no source on hand separates their split, by the search above. Clark and von Euler measured the whole response under hyperoxia, and hyperoxia does not silence the peripheral arm. Dahan 1990 (Summary 4 and 8, printed p. 485) split the response to end-tidal CO2 steps into a fast component, which it ascribes to the peripheral loop, and a slow one. Under hyperoxia the fast component averaged about 24% of the total CO2 sensitivity in three of nine men, was absent in three and doubtful in three, and Dahan concludes that the peripheral chemoreceptors in general add to ventilation under hyperoxia. Dahan recorded the breath's pattern (p. 487) and publishes the arms' responses in ventilation only. The default applies one split to the summed drive scale, as the body sums the arms today. Settling the split by arm needs rate and tidal volume under an isocapnic hypoxic step and a hyperoxic CO2 step, and a method that separates the arms' components, such as Dahan's, applied to rate and tidal volume.

Waking against NREM sleep: at rest on room air, tidal volume was lower asleep in all three studies, significantly in Douglas and in Gothe's stage II and not significantly in Stradling's 6 men; the change in rate was small and its direction differs by study (the table above, primary). These are stage means, not a drive moved in one person. Clark and von Euler's runs began below resting tidal volume, which let them see more of range 1 (p. 293), awake. Under CO2 in sleep, Gothe's abstract gives ventilation's response at 79% of waking on average and a smaller rise in mean inspiratory flow. It gives no rate or tidal volume under CO2, so no work on hand measures the split under a chemical step in sleep. Gothe 1981's full text would settle it if its results give rate and tidal volume under CO2; it cannot be had now.

In the engine, sleep is not a drive scale set under one. The scale is `C = rest - W s + (1 - L s) (arms)` (`crates/ng-nervous/src/chemoreflex.rs`, line 9, built at lines 72 to 75): asleep it loses the wake drive `W s`, each arm's gain falls by `L s` of itself, and the arms then answer the gases. With both gains zero, asleep it is `rest - W s` exactly. On body 11's sleeping hour run its breath means were 1.0254 awake and 0.9216, 0.9374, 0.9751 and 0.9967 at 5, 10, 30 and 60 minutes, as arterial carbon dioxide rose ([body ticket 11](../../body/issues/11-breathing-is-controlled.md), line 82). Under one, the ranges form below puts the change in depth with the rate held, and the linear form moves both.

### What bounds depth, and where rate takes the rest

Where rate joins, under the ranges form: at a tidal volume of 1.5 to 2 times its resting value, the end of range 1 (Clark and von Euler, Summary 5, p. 267; primary, 7 healthy men, hyperoxic rebreathing). The extent varied between men, and those who breathed fast at rest had a longer range 1 (pp. 280 to 281). The textbooks' inflation-reflex volumes, 1 L (West PDF p. 321) and about 1.5 L (Guyton 12e p. 506), are not the bend: Clark and von Euler measured where inspiration starts to shorten in man. Under the linear form rate moves at every drive.

What bounds depth: no work on hand measures a bound under a chemical drive. In range 2 depth and rate rise together, depth as about the square root of the drive under the ranges form (below). Range 2 was fitted to group means of inspiratory volume up to about 3.1 L in Fig. 6b's man, with the uppermost group, near 3.6 L, left out as range 3, and that man's single breaths reached about 4 L (Fig. 6, pp. 280 to 281, read from the plot). Range 3 is extra volume with a longer inspiration than range 2 predicts (p. 281). A run stopped at moderate discomfort or an end-tidal CO2 of 8% (p. 270), and the paper gives no drive for that point. So the ranges form above the fitted volumes, range 3 included, is an extrapolation. Tidal volume and inspiratory volume were usually equal up to 3 or 4 times the resting tidal volume, and above that tidal volume exceeded inspiratory volume by up to about 15% (p. 282). The only plateau the works on hand give is in exercise: tidal volume generally peaks around 50 to 60% of vital capacity, 1.9 to 2.7 L (Harbour 2022, a review, p. 3, citing Blackie 1991), and plateaus after about 55% of vital capacity (Chuang 2019 p. 1, citing a textbook chapter). Chuang's 19 older men reached 1.97 L, 0.57 of their slow vital capacity, at peak exercise (Table 2, p. 5). Exercise is a non-goal, so these give a likely magnitude for a bound and not a source for one under a chemical drive. The leads note says Hey 1966 found tidal volume rising with ventilation up to about half the vital capacity and then flat; no page on hand holds that (Gaps), so neither form below holds a ceiling.

The drives body 38 will run are not bounded here: body 38 asks the builder to measure the built loop, whether the drive scale swings, and an hour's run (body 38, lines 14 and 19). The earlier controller, on the rate alone, recorded these: the scale's breath means from 1.0266 to 1.0761 over the awake hour (body 11, line 71), 0.9216 to 0.9967 asleep (line 82), and one swing from 0.981 to 1.054 under depth at four times the gain (line 44). Body 38 records a mean of 1.03125 over its span from native-contract 100 (line 23). Its sleep law's test reaches 2.47365 on a fixture with a central gain of `48 / f64::EPSILON` (line 22), a numerical case and not a breath. None of these bounds the new build's drive at the instant.

### The split as a law can state it

Two model choices, neither made by the source, carry both forms to the engine's drive scale `C` (dimensionless, its admitted beat's mean one at the awake rest). Each is an inference, and the forms below name the ones they use:

- **Choice 1, the drive's measure.** The ranges form takes `C` as mean inspiratory flow `V_T / T_I` over its rest's. Clark and von Euler's model has the drive set how fast the lung fills (p. 268, Summary 9; p. 293), and Douglas 1982 takes the same flow as an index of inspiratory drive (PDF p. 4, citing Milic-Emili and Grunstein 1976), but neither calibrates the engine's `C` to it. The linear form takes `C` as ventilation over its rest's.
- **Choice 2, `p` zero.** With `p` kept, inspiration's share is `1 / (1 + n + p / T_I)` (above), the rate factor is not the rest's `T_I` over `T_I`, and ventilation is not mean inspiratory flow times a fixed share. The ranges form sets `p` to zero, so the share is `1 / (1 + n)` in both ranges, the rate factor is the rest's `T_I` over `T_I`, and `C` is also ventilation over its rest's. The linear form needs no timing law and so no `p`.

`R` is the rate factor and `D` the depth factor, each over the awake rest's, and `R D = C` in both forms.

**The ranges form, Clark and von Euler's** (inferred from Table 1, p. 288, with choices 1 and 2):

- `D(C) = C` and `R(C) = 1` up to the bend, `C <= b_V` (range 1: inspiration's length fixed).
- Above the bend, `D(C) = (a_V + sqrt(a_V^2 + 4 (b_V - a_V) C)) / 2` and `R(C) = C / D(C)` (range 2). This is Table 1's hyperbola `(V_T - V_0) T_I = C_h` in the resting breath's units: `D` is `V_T` over the resting tidal volume, `1 / R` is `T_I` over its rest's, `a_V` is `V_0` over the resting tidal volume, and continuity at the bend fixes `C_h` over the resting tidal volume times the resting `T_I` to `b_V - a_V`. With `a_V` zero, `D = sqrt(b_V C)` and `R = sqrt(C / b_V)`. Both factors are exactly one at rest, and `D_2(b_V) = b_V` for every `a_V` under `b_V`.
- It holds over the volumes range 2 was fitted on. Above them, and through range 3, it is an extrapolation (above).
- For the engine, `R(C) = smooth_max(1, C / D_2(C), w_V)`, the contract's smooth maximum, with `D_2` the range 2 expression, and `D(C) = C / R(C)`. `D_2` needs a square root: the contract's `Op` enum (`crates/ng-contract/src/law.rs` from line 310) holds `Exp`, `Ln`, `SmoothMax` and `SmoothMin` but no square root, power or hard maximum. The law can state the root of `x = a_V^2 + 4 (b_V - a_V) C`, positive for every positive `C`, as `exp(ln(x) / 2)`, or the builder adds a square root. Which one is body 38's call.
- The width moves both factors. In real arithmetic the smooth maximum exceeds both operands for a positive width, so `R` is above one at every `C` and `D` is under both `C` and `D_2(C)`. `D` and `R` are then factors on the hard form's rest, not on the smooth form's own. At `b_V` 1.5, `a_V` 0 and `w_V` 0.2: `R(1)` is 1.04396 and `D(1)` 0.95789, and at the bend `R` is `1 + w_V / 2`, 1.1, and `D(1.5)` is 1.36364, not 1.5. Below the bend `R` tends to one only as `w_V` tends to zero: at a fixed width, as `C` tends to zero, `R` tends to `(1 + sqrt(1 + w_V^2)) / 2` for a positive `a_V`, and `D` to zero. So `b_V` and `a_V` fitted against a measured tidal volume ratio are fitted against the form with its width.
- In binary64 a small positive width changes nothing. The contract evaluates `0.5 (a + b + sqrt((a - b)^2 + w^2))` (`crates/ng-contract/src/evaluate.rs`, lines 34 to 42), which returns the hard maximum once `w^2` is lost in the rounding of `(a - b)^2`, or `w` in that of `a + b` at the bend. At `b_V` 1.5 and `a_V` 0, a width up to about 6.7e-9 returns `R` and `D` exactly one at `C = 1`, and at the bend a width up to about 2.2e-16 returns `R` exactly one. Such a width keeps the awake rest's bits and leaves a corner at the bend, where native-contract 76 measured the smooth forms' small slope losing its digits. A wider width moves the awake rate by an amount the builder measures on the fixture.

**The linear form, Hey 1966's relation** (inferred from Clark and von Euler's statement of it, p. 291, with choice 1 as ventilation):

- Ventilation linear in tidal volume, `V_E = m (V_T - k)`, with `m` a rate (ventilation per tidal volume) and `k` a volume. With `C` the ventilation over its rest's and `h_V` the intercept `k` over the resting tidal volume, `D(C) = 1 + (1 - h_V) (C - 1)`, which is `h_V + (1 - h_V) C`, and `R(C) = C / D(C)`. `m` cancels.
- No bend, no square root and no width. Both factors are exactly one at rest, in binary64 too, since `C - 1` is zero there. Rate moves with the drive at every `C`: the slope of `R` at rest is `h_V`. As `C` grows, `R` tends to `1 / (1 - h_V)` and depth carries the rest; as `C` tends to zero, `D` tends to `h_V` and `R` to zero. `h_V` lies in `[0, 1)` for depth to stay positive at every positive drive, and at zero the whole change is depth.
- The relation is reported to end at a plateau of tidal volume, and no page on hand gives it (Gaps). Stating one would take a smooth minimum on `D` with its own width.

### What body 38 should know

The engine today, in `crates/ng-nervous/src/breath.rs`:

- The drive scale speeds the phase in both inspiration and expiration: line 103, `k.scale(rate, scale)`, inside the loop over `[&inspiration, &expiration]` at line 100. The guards end each phase at a fixed phase distance (`ns.inspiration-length` and `ns.expiration-length`, lines 56 and 58), so one phase speed fixes the two phases' distances, not the share of elapsed time. The share stays fixed for a drive held constant over the breath, which the sleep studies support at rest and which choice 2 assumes under rebreathing. A drive higher through inspiration than through expiration shortens inspiration's time more and lowers its share, and the reverse raises it: with the packet's distances 0.4 and 0.6, a speed doubled through inspiration alone gives a share of 0.25 against 0.4.
- The pump drive relaxes toward `ns.drive-peak` (line 59) at the speed `ns.drive-rise` (line 50, the rising law at lines 88 to 93) and toward zero at `ns.drive-fall` (line 51, lines 94 to 98). A shorter inspiration reaches a lower drive, so depth falls slightly as rate rises.

The candidate for how depth moves is the peak: `ns.drive-peak` times `D(C)`, set at each breath edge, with the phase speed times `R(C)`. Body 38 names native-contract 87's form, a firing at each breath edge that sets the pump drive's peak from the drive scale, as its first candidate (body 38, line 9), and native-contract 87 asks for that form and leaves its cost to measure. At fixed timings the drive's repeating waveform is linear in its peak, so the drive at inspiration's end scales by `D` exactly, whether or not it nears the peak within inspiration. That is the neural drive and not the volume: transmitter release, reuptake and binding at the pump's junction, filling through the airway's conductance and the recoil set tidal volume, all in elapsed time. The packet's breath calculation (`crates/ng-validate/src/body/packet.rs`, from line 1470) steps the junction and the mechanics at lines 1494 to 1505. So tidal volume against `C` is read on the fixture against `D(C)`, under either form.

The alternative is the rise, which is the source's mechanism: the drive sets how fast the lung fills (Clark and von Euler p. 268, Summary 9; p. 293). Eckert (PDF p. 565) and Ganong (PDF p. 1503) give the qualitative effect at textbook tier, from animals, and no equation for this engine. The engine's drive relaxes toward its peak, so a faster rise moves the drive at inspiration's end by far less than the scale. Over the packet's 2 s inspiration at a rise of 1.5 a second, from a drive of zero, a rise 1.2 times faster moves the drive at inspiration's end from 0.95021 to 0.97268 of the peak, a factor of 1.02364 against the 1.2 asked, and from 0.95033 to 0.97274 on the repeating breath. A rise alone cannot carry depth; it would need the peak as well. Scaling both `ns.drive-rise` and `ns.drive-fall` by `R(C)` keeps the drive's waveform in phase at a held peak, and it does not keep tidal volume, since the junction and the airway run in elapsed time.
